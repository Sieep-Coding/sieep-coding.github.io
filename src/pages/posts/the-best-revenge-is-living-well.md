# The Best Revenge Is Living Well

> Vivere bene est optima vindicta.

I've heard the phrase "the best [revenge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revenge) is living well" my whole life. It always struck me as a nice sentiment without teeth behind it. Live well and somehow the person who wronged you suffers? 

That never fully made sense to me until I started thinking about what 'living well' required to work as revenge in the first place: **an audience.**

---

## Where the Phrase Comes From

You'll primarily see it credited to [George Herbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Herbert), the 17th century [Welsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wales) [poet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poet), whose 1651 collection of [proverbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proverb) *Jacula Prudentum* contains a version of it. Other historians trace it further back to Roman moralists and [Stoic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoicism)-adjacent thinking, the same intellectual neighborhood as [Seneca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seneca_the_Younger) and his writings on [anger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anger)/grudges.

Its exact origin is murky, which is fitting for a phrase that's now essentially [folklore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folklore).

What's consistent across every version of it is the implication of being *seen*. Living well as revenge only functions if the person you're getting revenge on can [observe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observation) the living. For most of human history, that observation happened slowly. A [wedding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wedding) a few towns over, a promotion mentioned secondhand at [church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_(building)). This information moved at the speed of [gossip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gossip), which is to say, not very fast.

Do you remember as a child playing that [telephone game](https://www.wikihow.com/Telephone-Game-Phrases) to teach you about spreading information? Usually by the second or third person, the message would be entirely different.

---

## The Problem: "Living Well"

[Social media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_media) turned "living well" from something you quietly did into something you constantly [performed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Performativity). The [vacation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacation) isn't real until it's posted. The revenge isn't revenge unless someone specific saw the [story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instagram#Features) and, ideally, [screenshotted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screenshot) it and sent it to someone else.

The issue is that this flips the entire [premise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Premise) of the old proverb on its head. The original idea wasn't "make sure they see how good you're doing." It was closer to "don't waste your [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_(psychological)) on them at all, just go live your life, and if it gets back to them, so be it."

'Oversharing culture' has trained an entire [generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generation) to believe that a life not [documented](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Documentation) is a life not lived. I'd argue the opposite. If you have to prove you're doing well on some level you're still existing for the person you're trying to prove it to. This kind of [rumination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rumination_(psychology)) is not worth it.  That's still giving them rent-free space in your head, just with better [lighting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lighting) and a filter.

---

## Doubt Is the Actual Weapon

Here's the part I think gets missed. In a world where everyone is [oversharing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-disclosure) absence isn't neutral.

If someone who used to post disappears, the people who wronged them are left with nothing. No proof of [misery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suffering). No proof of [triumph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triumph). Give them [silence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silence), because silence is a lot harder to interpret than a [reel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short-form_content).

Someone posting about their life gives an [ex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Significant_other), a former friend, an old boss, a clean [narrative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narrative) to file away.

In fact, the Zeigarnik effect is a psychological idea that an individual remembers unfinished or uninterrupted tasks better than completed tasks.

---

## What This Actually Looks Like

If you stop needing an audience to [validate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emotional_validation) that your life is going well then the people who once had a front row seat simply lose their seat. You stopped selling them [tickets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ticket_(admission)).

I've written before about [why I choose to subscribe instead of follow](/posts/I-Dont-Follow-I-Subscribe) and this is the other half of the same coin. Cutting the [feeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/News_feed) out protects your own [headspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_space). It quietly removes you from being a [data point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_record_data) in someone else's story. No one can screenshot a life you didn't [publish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Publishing).

---

## Conclusion

The way I read the old proverb, it was not about [winning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Competition). It was about [disengaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disengagement_theory) completely so that winning and losing stop being the frame. The version of that idea suited for right now isn't a well-lit photo captioned with a [subtweet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tweet_(social_media)). 

Close the [app](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_app), go and do something worth doing for its own sake. Doubt is [patient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patience).
